# B — Two whole-Series concepts and the synthesis

Status: **working / noncanonical**. Nothing here is accepted Identity, Structure, or Book canon. The fixed author brief is the only fixed input; every name, engine and arc below is a proposal.

The two concepts are compared on **generative causal organization**: how many engines govern, how a choice becomes a changed state, and what kind of pressure the changed state produces. Names, settings and aesthetics are not the difference. Both concepts share the fixed brief (epic fantasy; wonder, belonging and consequential relationships; victories that change the world; no escalating antagonists; local closure plus long-range questions).

---

## Concept α — "Debt of Hands" (layered: one governing mechanism)

**Premise shape.** In a terraced highland country, every gift of water or protection is recorded as a debt that the receiver must repay in labour. The whole social order is an inherited web of these debts. One institution keeps the record.

**Reader promise.** Every kindness is visible as a debt, and the reader watches debts reprice across generations: who owes, who was credited, who can finally refuse.

**Principal generative mechanism (single governing engine).** *Recorded debt.* Choice: honour, transfer, forgive or reread a debt. Consequence: creditors and debtors reposition. Changed state: the ledger's legitimacy and arithmetic change. New pressure: the new arithmetic generates a new set of unpaid or unpayable obligations.

**Subordinate elements.** A physical source of water responds passively to the total of debts. A hidden older script is a single eventual reveal about the ledger's origin.

**How the world changes through outcomes.** Each victory reprices debts. A forgiven debt makes a neighbour a free party; a free party then borrows; borrowing creates a new creditor class.

**Why future Books keep producing conflicts.** The ledger always has a next arithmetic problem, and every repricing creates a new creditor and a new debtor.

**Main risk.** *Repetition at the grammar level.* Every Book becomes another repricing of the same relation. Conflicts differ in who owes whom, not in what kind of causal pressure they bring. The physical world remains scenery. The hidden script is a revelation device that tends toward postponement, which the brief forbids.

## Concept β — "The Spring Ledger" (ensemble: three co-governing engines with rotating governance)

**Premise shape.** A highland valley is watered from a glacier-fed spring. Water reaches the terraces only where its shares are recorded, and each recorded share creates a labour-return owed to the channels. Three systems interact: the record, the spring's physical balance, and an older script that governs what a share legally and morally *is*.

**Reader promise.** Wonder at a responsive physical order; belonging through shared return labour; and relationships whose obligations change meaning across generations.

**Principal generative mechanisms (three engines, no single centre).**
1. *Ledger* (accounting): recorded shares and owed returns.
2. *Source* (physical commons): the spring's balance rises or falls with the gap between draw and return. Thinning and surfeit are physical consequences, not character choices.
3. *Old Hand* (interpretive legitimacy): the readings of the oldest script decide whether a return is a debt, a gift or a contract.

Causal loops, stated so they are not merely coexisting:
- **L1 (Ledger ↔ Source).** Recorded shares set the draw. The spring's balance then decides whether the recorded shares can physically be honoured. Feasibility is tested every season.
- **L2 (Old Hand → Ledger → Source).** A reading changes what a share is. That changes whether returns are recorded or given freely. The change in return alters the spring's balance.
- **L3 (openness → trade → Source).** Publishing the records makes shares tradable. Tradability separates draw from return. Separation produces thinning.

**How the world changes through outcomes.** Distribution of draw and return changes, and so do the meanings of both. Each arc gives a different engine the governing role, so the centre of gravity moves. The series' central question is therefore not "who wins" but "who gives back the water, and must giving be counted?"

**Why future Books keep producing new conflicts.** Each engine produces a different *kind* of pressure. The Ledger produces distributional disputes; the Source produces feasibility and physical risk; the Old Hand produces legitimacy disputes. A victory in one engine creates pressure in another, so the sequence does not require a new antagonist.

**Main risk.** *Fragmentation.* Three engines may simply coexist, each generating its own Books with no causal exchange. The series would then read as a committee of subplots. Its complexity also makes the author's workload heavier.

## Comparison (qualitative)

| Question | α — Debt of Hands | β — Spring Ledger |
|---|---|---|
| Causal parsimony | High: one chain of consequence | Lower: three engines must be kept in exchange |
| Non-character causal system | Weak: the physical world is passive | Strong: the Source's balance is a real constraint |
| Renewal grammar | One grammar (repricing), so repetition risk is high | Three grammars (distribution, feasibility, legitimacy) |
| Independent agency | Institutions tend to be subordinated to the ledger | Institutions can act from their own engine (e.g., trade, dissent) |
| Revelation risk | The hidden script is a single late reveal | The script can be staged as partial readings across Books |
| Fragmentation risk | Low | High, but mitigated only if L1–L3 are actually exercised |
| Fit to fixed brief: "victories change the world," "no arbitrary new threats" | Repricing can create debts that feel arbitrary | Physical feasibility limits what new pressure can be |

Neither concept is objectively better. α is easier to explain and holds together with fewer moving parts. β better satisfies the fixed brief's demands for independent agency, a non-character causal system, and renewal that does not rely on new antagonists.

## Selected direction: β, with two constraints borrowed from α

**Selected:** β, the ensemble of three engines with rotating governance.

**Borrowed from α:**
1. Every macro arc must show one visible repricing of who owes return. This keeps the Ledger's debt grammar as the surface of the record engine rather than letting it dissolve into the Source's physics.
2. The Old Hand's readings are scheduled as partial readings (Book 1 open, Book 8 partial, Book 12 partial, Book 15 complete) so the script is never a revelation deferred indefinitely.

**Why β won.** The fixed brief requires a non-character causal system, several agents with independence, transformation across generations, and renewal without escalation. α can attempt these, but only by letting the ledger carry every pressure, which is the repetition risk that the renewal test in step 4 is meant to expose. β's cost, fragmentation, is controlled by naming three explicit loops (L1–L3) that each arc must exercise. The author can still reject β; α remains the fallback if the three-engine organization proves too heavy in the Studio candidate.

**Not decided.** The protagonist's identity, the lowland towns' fate, whether any terrace keeps a measured ledger at the end, and the ontology of the Source. These are recorded as open questions in the working architecture (A).
