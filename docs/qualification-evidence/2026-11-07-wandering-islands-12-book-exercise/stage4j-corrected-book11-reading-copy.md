# Beyond the Written Sea

### I. The Place Where the Rain Went

On *Kindling Tide*, rain arrived inside before it arrived outside.

The children knew because the sailcloth gardens would begin to whisper. They ran the length of the upper deck with cups, trying to catch the first drops shaken loose from the netting overhead, while the adults pretended not to notice that half the cups had been stolen from the breakfast table. The great woven troughs hung between the cabin roofs like long, green-bellied hammocks. Mint grew beside salt flowers; purple beans climbed the stays; a pear tree no taller than a child had been tied into a wooden cradle so that the sea could rock it without pulling up its roots.

Today the whisper had the wrong sound.

Water struck the deck in a steady, accusatory patter. It was not raining hard enough for so much of it to be falling through. Nemi followed the noise past a woman gutting fish with the authority of someone who had done it a thousand times, and found Ivo kneeling beneath the oldest herb bed. A copper collar at its lower seam had gone green and split. The water collected in a bright stream and fell directly through a gap between the planks.

Ivo had placed a bucket beneath it. The bucket was nearly full.

“It's feeding the fish,” Nemi offered.

“Wonderful. We can invite the fish to breakfast and ask whether they prefer thyme.”

The herb bed had belonged to Pera before anyone could remember Pera being young. Its leaves scented the evening meal and, when a child had a fever, the little room where somebody sat beside them. Now Pera stood on the other side of the trough, feeling along the seam with a finger. Her granddaughter Liri held a plate beneath a second leak, looking delighted by the abundance of emergencies.

“How much binding?” Pera asked.

“Enough that we have to take it from somewhere else,” Ivo said.

“The window bed?”

Ivo did not answer.

Liri looked up. “That's where the red flowers live.”

“They'd live in another bed,” Pera told her.

“They wouldn't know which one was theirs.”

Pera's mouth softened. She gave the child the injured collar to hold, then caught it back before the cracked rim cut her palm. A proper replacement would save the cloth. A temporary patch would have to be made from something that could withstand both salt and movement. They had already borrowed wire from the fishing tackle and ruined two good needles. Everyone aboard understood the arithmetic. That did not make anybody eager to take apart the window bed, where three generations had planted herbs for the daily soup.

Nemi crouched beside the bucket. “I remember the metalworkers in the familiar basin. Their collars opened at the side. They could change one without emptying the whole bed.”

“You remember a man who fixed a hinge while you told a story,” Ivo said.

“A woman. She fixed the hinge and the handle. I watched.”

“You watched her hands, or her face?”

“Her hands.”

“Then why do you describe her smile whenever you tell it?”

Pera laughed so suddenly that Liri spilled her plate. Nemi accepted the point. The trouble with having once brought the first reliable news of a living island was that people assumed one had subsequently learned everything about the sea. Nemi had stood at foreign docks while strangers wept and called their name. Now they could not tell a water-catching collar from a hinge without being corrected by a person half their age.

“There's an opportunity,” Nemi said. “If a near-side maker can be reached this circuit—”

“There's a whole sea between an opportunity and a collar,” Pera said.

“I know.”

“You know a sea. Tava knows which bit we might sail.”

Tava was at the lower rail, listening without appearing to. They had been scraping salt from a sounding weight, an ugly piece of lead that Nemi had once described as Tava's favorite ornament. Tava turned it in their hands.

“Lacebank was there when I checked,” they said. “Our cutter can reach it under the conditions we have now. Past it, I haven't promised anything. And I won't take the home ferry on a shopping expedition.”

“We don't need the home ferry,” Nemi said quickly. “We could take *Mothwake*.”

“Could,” Tava said. “If we leave enough people here, and if the stores belong to the cutter's outing, not to everyone else's next passage. And if the person whose work you plan to trade wants to trade it.”

Ivo looked up from the bucket.

Nemi knew what was coming. The prototype Ivo had woven lay dry in the workroom, its loops built to shed water inward even when the cloth around it stretched. Ivo had promised it to the gardeners who had raised them. It was not an object to be borrowed like a wooden spoon.

“I was going to ask,” Nemi said.

“After you'd decided where it was going?”

“Before I took it.”

“There's a difference.” Ivo climbed to their feet. “You talk about our gardens when you want to go somewhere, and your expedition when someone asks who will sit at the loom all night.”

The words were sharper than Ivo intended; they looked toward Pera, who pretended to be more interested in the broken collar than in the argument. Nemi stood too. For a moment the only sound was the leak. It struck the deck with an irritating cheerfulness.

“Then tell me what I can help make,” Nemi said.

Ivo's expression altered. “Help?”

“I know how to tie a useful knot.”

“You know how to tie knots that hold a boat to its own mooring until somebody else comes and unties them.”

“That is still a kind of usefulness.”

Ivo considered the bucket, their hands, the other work waiting upstairs. At last they said, “I could make a second sleeve. A small one. A sample, not the promised one. The old family weave stays here. If anyone wants to copy the new loop, they can ask me, not you.”

Nemi agreed before Ivo could improve the terms. Tava, who had been quiet, finally walked over.

“I'll take three people. Nemi, because they know what they want to find. Ivo, because the sample is theirs. Me, because one of us must know when not to sail. We check Lacebank again at departure. If the next water isn't known, we come back.”

“What if a repair maker is just beyond where you can go?” asked Liri.

“We will have met the sea at an inconvenient place,” Tava answered. “That's not new.”

She was disappointed by how ordinary the answer sounded. Tava let her hold the lead weight, which was heavier than she expected. When she handed it back, she asked if a weight could tell them where an island would be tomorrow.

“No,” Tava said. “It tells me what is below us now. That's enough to make it useful.”

They made the second sleeve through the afternoon. Nemi wound thread, badly at first. Ivo made them unwind it and begin again. Pera brought soup for everyone and moved a pot to catch another leak. By lamplight the little sample had a pale edge that could be loosened while the rest stayed stretched. Nemi thought it beautiful; Ivo thought one of its loops was wrong.

They corrected it without asking for praise.

Before dawn, the crew of *Kindling Tide* stood on both decks to watch *Mothwake* push clear. Liri gave Ivo two dried red flowers wrapped in a cloth, one for the journey and one to bring back. Pera did not wave. She was holding the damaged bed upright while someone fetched another bucket.

Nemi noticed. They had wanted to sail toward something. The people remaining had to stay and keep a home from leaking.

### II. A Line Drawn in Moving Water

At Lacebank, the sea made a pocket of quiet beneath a wall of pale stone.

Tava had seen it from the outer side two days before. That earlier visit had not guaranteed the pocket would still exist; its stone stood on a moving island, and the wind had turned since then. Tava waited offshore until the current settled enough to show the sheltered water, sounded twice, and brought *Mothwake* in with room to turn.

Nemi had hoped the passage would feel like an adventure. Mostly it felt like carrying water without spilling it. Tava would not let them stand where their excitement cast a shadow across the sounding marks. Ivo took the forward line and learned to call the weight back without tangling it.

They ate hard bread in the stone's lee. The little sample sleeve hung between two poles to dry. A gull descended to inspect it, screamed as though offended by the price, and left. For a while the best event of the expedition was Nemi's attempt to catch a fish that had no interest in being caught.

Then Ivo pointed through the opening.

“Is that a roof?”

Beyond the reef, at a distance the gull could have crossed more readily than *Mothwake*, something red lifted above a cluster of bright roofs. The red shape fell and rose with the wind. Nemi shaded their eyes. There were boats gathered beneath it, and thin columns of cooking smoke. A repair pennant, perhaps. Somewhere people were working.

“We could ask,” Nemi said.

“We can ask from here,” Tava replied.

They brought the cutter to the last water Tava could presently justify. The lead found a gentle slope, then a sudden change: sound enough to sail, perhaps, but the current along the outer cut had begun to comb foam into pale threads. Tava sent Nemi back to the rudder and told Ivo not to stand up.

“On my hand, turn us,” they said.

“Why? The roofs are there.”

“The roofs aren't the bottom.”

The red pennant moved between the shrouds. Tava sounded again, waited for the cutter's movement to match what the line told them, and drew back toward the safe turning water. They did not speak for several breaths. The boat creaked softly around them.

Nemi hated the stillness that followed. They could smell cooking fires from the far dock now. The sea had brought them close enough to imagine hot bread, and no closer.

A bell rang from the roofs. It rang twice, paused, then rang again. A small near-side boat pushed clear of the dock, barely visible between the red pennant and a low black reef.

“They've seen us,” Ivo said.

“They've seen a cutter,” Tava said. “That's not permission to enter.”

Nemi tied their trade cloth to a pole and held it up: an open palm sewn above a little cup. They had used the sign at outer markets to mean *we want to exchange, if you do*. The near-side boat stopped rather than advancing. Its pilot raised a strip of white cloth, then lowered it, and began working a line into the water.

Tava smiled very slightly. “Good.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means somebody on that side is asking the bottom a question before answering us.”

The near-side pilot approached only within their own known water, sounding, then marking a checked edge with a small float. The cutter remained outside its last confirmed turning mark. From that distance Nemi could see only a woman in a dark cap, one hand at the line and one on her little boat's gunwale. When she called, the words arrived broken by wind.

Tava made Nemi lower the trade pole. Ivo leaned forward and caught the next call: not a welcome but a warning about a shallow patch that rose and vanished under foam. The near-side pilot sounded back toward it, then across to a stretch Tava had already checked from the opposite direction.

At first the calls disagreed.

“We have deeper water to starboard,” Tava shouted.

“You're reading from the wrong side of my mark,” the woman called back. “The current's dragging your bow.”

Tava looked again, then had Nemi ease the rudder. The cutter had shifted sideways while Nemi stared at the pennant. The float was not where they had assumed it stood relative to the reef. Tava took a new sounding rather than argue with someone who could see a different face of the water.

“You were right,” Tava called.

The woman's answer came with a grin. “I sometimes am.”

There was no grand unveiling of a road through the sea. There were two small boats, two wet ropes, a shifting band of foam, and an increasing number of places that both pilots had actually checked. Tava advanced the cutter along their own supported reach, stopping wherever retreat remained possible. The woman came from the dock side, keeping her own turning water behind her. They hailed across a narrowing strip of sea until each could describe the same weed-dark patch and the same stone tooth that broke the surface at irregular intervals.

Nemi saw Tava's shoulders settle. That meant more than the smile had.

“Here,” Tava said. “The water she's just sounded is the water we sounded coming in, from the other side.”

“Can we go?”

“Not yet. We check whether we can turn if the current changes while we're there.”

Nemi was about to protest that the dock looked no farther than a thrown apple. At that moment the water beneath the cutter swayed, and the stone tooth showed another inch of its pale face. Somewhere beneath them a whole island was continuing its ordinary motion without regard for three people who wanted copper.

The other pilot called a new warning. Tava sent Ivo to check the line again. Ivo was frightened enough to do it perfectly. The lead came up wet and cold, and when it touched their wrist they could feel their own pulse beneath the rope.

The woman brought her boat along the near-side edge and showed Tava a reach she had freshly sounded, including a place where a small cutter could turn without touching stone. Tava checked the overlapping stretch from their side, then asked her name.

“Arlen,” she called.

“Tava.”

“I won't bring a large hull through,” Arlen said. “Not today. Your cutter could fit. The dock will take you if you want to trade.”

“And if we can't leave when we want to?”

“There's room to wait inside. You'll have to ask the keeper what she can spare you. I can't promise the reef will keep still.”

That honesty made the dock seem more real than its roofs had. Tava looked at the water barrels, the sample sleeve and the protected return stores. Then they asked Nemi, quietly, whether a single collar would still be worth the trip if that was all they could find.

Nemi had been rehearsing an answer about four.

“Yes,” they said.

Tava nodded to Ivo. “And you?”

“I want to see what a person who works copper thinks of my cloth.”

At the dock, children had gathered around the bell. One of them was waving so energetically that a grown woman had to catch his sleeve before he fell in. Nemi waved back. It was an extravagant gesture from so far away, useless as navigation and perfectly intelligible as greeting.

Tava gave Arlen the decision. She eased her own boat toward the shared checked water. *Mothwake* followed, not as if entering a corridor that would always be there, but as one living boat accepting the limited help of another. When the reef fell astern and the dock opened around them, Nemi discovered they had been holding their breath long enough for the first smell of hot bread to make their eyes sting.

The woman who took the mooring looked at their sails with curiosity, not awe.

“You're welcome to berth while this entrance is open,” she said. “The dock's called Rillturn. What do you make?”

Nemi pointed to Ivo.

“Ask the maker,” they said.

### III. What One Collar Could Buy

Rillturn's roofs were low and broad, built to shed rain toward stone jars rather than down onto the people passing underneath. Small metal scraps hung above the repair stalls. Wind made them chime against one another, so that a person walking the market heard a thousand imperfect bells instead of the single copper bowl used on *Kindling Tide* to announce supper.

The stall nearest the water sold lengths of cord. The next repaired hinges, kettle handles, toys, raincatching troughs, and the delicate wire cages people fastened around young trees. A boy held out a wooden bird whose wings moved when he pulled its string. Ivo pulled it twice, laughed with such open delight that Nemi forgot for a moment the bargain they had come to make, and bought it with a tiny shell button.

“Now we have less to trade,” Nemi said.

“Now we have a bird.”

“Which repairs nothing.”

“Not everything has to repair something.”

They reached the workshop with the smell of heated copper in their clothes. Its keeper, Eda, was not the metalworker. She kept the dry shelf, recorded repair orders, settled disputes about what had been promised and made people take their wet boots off the table. She looked from Ivo's wrapped sample to Tava's hands, saw the marks left by the sounding rope, and asked first whether they had somewhere safe to wait if the water changed.

Tava said Arlen had offered a berth.

“A berth, yes,” Eda replied. “But don't expect me to feed a ship for a fortnight.”

“It's a cutter,” Tava said.

“Then you may disappoint me on a smaller scale.”

Behind her, the repair maker lifted her head from a little forge. Her name was Marra; an old burn lay across one wrist. She wiped her hands and listened to Nemi describe the damaged trough collars aboard *Kindling Tide*. The more Nemi talked, the more Marra watched Ivo rather than Nemi.

“How many?” Nemi asked at last.

“How many what?”

“Collars you can spare.”

“Today? One.”

Nemi had expected four. They had rehearsed ways to say *please* in languages Marra had never heard, and none of them contained the word *one*.

“Four would keep the gardens intact through the next work circuit.”

“And four would leave three families here waiting for their own repairs.” Marra pointed to the chalk marks above her bench. “That family has a cistern that drips into their sleeping room. That one has a child's washbasin that bends. I can mend their work or sell all of it to strangers. If you can tell me what happens to the people whose orders I break, I'll consider it.”

Nemi looked at the marks. They were not anonymous, though Nemi could not read their names.

“One,” Ivo said. “We came to see whether any were possible.”

“And what have you brought?” Marra asked.

Ivo unwrapped the sleeve.

Marra touched the adjustable loop with one stained fingertip, then fetched a trough from a stack of things that had not yet been made useful. Its seam had been cut by a shard of metal. She put a cup beneath it.

“Show me,” she said.

Ivo spread the sample. It was beautiful in the way a useful thing could be beautiful: nothing embroidered, no costly dye, just hundreds of patient decisions about where water should go. Nemi held one edge while Ivo tightened another. Marra poured a dipper of water onto it.

Most of the water ran into the trough. A bright thread escaped at the corner and fell onto Nemi's shoe.

Marra looked pleased. Ivo looked furious.

“Not you,” Marra said. “That old seam doesn't lie flat. Come here.”

She turned the trough and showed them where its metal edging pulled against the cloth. It was a minute fault; Ivo would have blamed the weave for it. Marra slipped a small wedge beneath the worn edge and set the copper collar beside the sample.

“If you leave room here,” she said, tapping the rim, “the fabric can move without tearing. Your loop is doing more than the metal. But the metal has to let it.”

Ivo watched, then loosened the loop and rewove it around the temporary wedge. Marra steadied the trough. For several moments they stopped speaking altogether. Nemi watched their hands moving around a problem neither person could solve as quickly alone.

The second dipper went where it should. When Marra tipped the trough to one side, the sleeve still held its shape. When she loosened the collar, Ivo could remove the wet edge without emptying the whole bed.

Eda gave a low whistle. “You could change one patch while the plants stayed in place.”

“It's not ready to take the weight of a whole pear bed,” Ivo cautioned.

“Then we'll start with mint,” Marra said.

They grinned at one another. Nemi felt a sharp, unexpected jealousy: here was an exchange they had imagined arranging, and the most important part of it had occurred while Nemi held one end of a wet cloth.

Marra pushed the copper collar across the bench. Ivo did not take it yet.

“The sample is mine,” they said. “I made it separately from my family's old weave. You can use this loop and show another maker how to repair it. You can't call our private pattern yours, and I won't give you our sheltered berths.”

Marra scratched her scar with her thumb. “I want the knot. I don't want your beds, and I don't want to wake in a strange place with a stranger's route on my wall.”

“Some people would pay to know,” Nemi said.

“I know.” Marra looked toward Eda's shelf. “People have tried to pay me for their neighbor's orders, too. Money isn't always the difficult part.”

The trade was made. One collar for the working sleeve and its demonstrated adjustment. Ivo corrected the sketch Marra made of the loop and wrote their name on the page. Marra added a mark beside it showing the loosened metal edge; both agreed that anyone repairing the sample would need both details.

A child from the next stall arrived asking whether the wooden bird could be fixed. Ivo showed Marra the little toy, which had already lost one wing. Marra rummaged for a pin, put it through the joint and handed the bird back. It flapped better than before.

“You've got expensive habits,” Nemi said.

“It's not a habit,” Ivo said, flying the bird above the forge. “It's research.”

Tava had been watching the water from the open door. They turned at Ivo's laugh, and for the first time since leaving *Kindling Tide* their shoulders seemed to lose the weight of the voyage.

Eda brought lunch on a board. There was bread, a soft white cheese, and little dark plums with the salt still on their skins from someone's hands. She did not present it as a ceremony of welcome. She wanted to know whether there was a neat way to hang a garden bed from the roof without shaking the jars beneath it. Ivo drew an answer in spilled flour. Marra objected. Nemi contributed an entirely useless story about a boat that had once been mistaken for a whale.

Someone laughed hard enough to choke on a plum.

The sea continued changing outside. They could not stay to perfect the design. And while they ate, Eda's records lay drying on the table, with a new name among them that had never appeared in Rillturn before.

### IV. The Things a Chart Might Keep

The argument began over a piece of clean paper.

Arlen came into the workshop carrying a folded strip on which she had drawn the day's safe approach from Rillturn. She laid it beside Ivo's revised sleeve sketch. Both showed marks made by people who knew what they were doing. Both could be useful to somebody else.

“Make me a copy of the part you checked from your side,” Arlen said to Tava. “I'll keep them together.”

Tava looked at the page. “Until when?”

“Until we need to know how you came in.”

“And when somebody else needs to know how to come in?”

“Then I tell them when I saw it. Or I sound it again, if I can.”

A man carrying a basket of clean copper filings paused in the doorway. His name was Pell, and he supplied several near-side markets with repair stock. He had come to collect parts, but the sight of the unfamiliar cutter beyond the window had already distracted him from his orders.

“That's worth keeping,” he said. “A record of the first boat through. People could pay for a copy.”

“People can pay you for an old depth mark,” Arlen said, “if you're willing to tell them why it's old.”

Pell smiled without being offended. “I sell metal to families who can't afford to send someone out to ask the sea every morning. If a sketch helps them choose where to try, it has value. You aren't the only one who can judge what a buyer may use.”

Nemi understood him. A family aboard *Kindling Tide* might choose whether to cut apart an herb bed differently if they knew of a likely market for collars. If information reached them at all, it might make life easier. Yet the outer ferry's shelters did not stand still; publishing an attractive line from Lacebank to Rillturn could send a large boat into water that *Mothwake* had barely managed today.

Tava traced one of Arlen's marks with a finger.

“I'll say what I checked,” they said. “I'll even write what I refused. But your drawing stops at your observations. Mine stops at mine. Where the two met was true for our cutter today. Nobody buys tomorrow from me.”

Pell shifted his basket from one arm to another. “I'm not asking for tomorrow. I'm asking for a record of something that actually happened.”

“Then take the fact that it happened,” Nemi said. “What you can't take is a promise from us that the same water will be there when a buyer arrives.”

Pell's patience thinned. “A fact nobody can use is a decoration.”

“No,” Arlen said. “It's a warning someone might heed.”

That was the wrong thing to say to a man who sold things people needed. He looked toward the dock, where his workers were loading copper on a familiar trade craft, and asked why near-side pilots were paid from public stores if they intended to keep useful observations to themselves.

Arlen stood abruptly. “Because somebody has to tell the next boat when the last sounding expires. That's work, too.”

For a moment the room felt narrower than *Mothwake*.

Ivo took the sleeve drawing out from beneath the chart. “I know what you're afraid of losing,” they said to Pell. “I couldn't have found this collar without people who told me where a market was. But if you sell my family's sleeping place as though it were a repair shop, somebody will arrive needing what we didn't offer.”

Pell looked down at the corrected loop and Marra's sketch beside it. “You think I want to own your bed?”

“No,” Ivo said. “I think you want to help more customers than you can see. So do we.”

Marra fetched a scrap of paper and made a copy of the adjustable sleeve repair, marking Ivo's name. She handed that part to Pell. Arlen kept the dated approach observations in the pilot record, without treating them as a passage to the outer home's private shelters. Pell could describe the visit in his trade account, but Tava would not certify a route for sale.

Pell accepted the repair drawing. He was not satisfied, and he was not pretending to be. A family unable to pay for a skilled pilot would remain at a disadvantage. The room had not solved that problem.

Eda, who had listened while checking the shelf's water damage, said, “If you mean to argue about promises, I have one expiring.”

She took a waxed envelope from the locked dry drawer.

Nemi's hand went still. A small mark on the outside named Larkhome. The handwriting belonged to nobody Nemi recognized. There had been years when the name of that living island could make a whole dock fall quiet. Now it was written on a real packet with a damaged corner and a custody date.

“This came by two willing crews,” Eda said. “The first took it to a funded station. The second brought repair goods to us. The last pilot stopped because she could not sound the farther cut. I agreed to keep it until an ordinary return opportunity, but that isn't an endless obligation.”

“Who is it from?” Nemi asked.

“I don't know. I have not opened it. The outside asks for a willing hand only as far as a person can actually carry it.”

Tava looked at the water beyond the window. “We have enough stores for home under the conditions we're checking. We do not have a voyage to Larkhome.”

“I know,” Nemi said.

Ivo had been turning the repaired wooden bird between their fingers. “And if we take it home, it becomes our work. Who tells the family it has not arrived?”

“Whoever next carries the receipt back. If there is a safe carrier.”

“So we could hold it for a year?”

“We could,” Nemi said, and wished the word had felt less large.

Eda set the envelope on the table without pushing it toward them. “If you decline, I need to return it under the terms I promised. That has its own cost. I would prefer a willing outer carrier, but a preference isn't a claim.”

Nemi thought of the crowd that had greeted them when they brought word that Larkhome was alive. They had watched people mistake gratitude for a contract, as if a successful trip meant Nemi would be able to repeat it whenever needed. They had enjoyed the praise. Some part of them still did.

They turned to Tava. “The cutter returns to *Kindling Tide* if the water allows. Nothing beyond.”

“Yes,” Tava said.

Nemi turned to Ivo. “I'll keep the custody record myself and answer for returning the letter if another hand never comes. I won't make the gardeners store it or lend our travel water for it.”

Ivo studied them. “You can't promise where the island will be when you want to return it.”

“No.”

“Then promise what you can do when the problem is yours.”

“I'll tell the truth about where it rests. If we can't send it on or back, I'll keep its condition and its history. It won't vanish into my reputation.”

Ivo considered the old wooden bird, then set it beside the envelope. “All right. But you have to show Pera the custody terms. It isn't only your ship.”

Eda wrote the record carefully: received by Nemi aboard *Mothwake*, onward destination unconfirmed, Larkhome not reached, return or further offer only under future willing and observed conditions. Tava signed for the possibility of a safe homeward journey, not for delivery. Nemi signed for custody. Nobody opened the wax.

Outside, Arlen called that she had new observations from the entrance. The sea had not paused for their conversation.

### V. One Thing Repaired

They could not leave immediately.

The water at the outer turning mark had changed enough that Arlen wanted another sounding before she would guide them through it. The dock keeper offered a place to wait. Tava accepted without resentment and then spent the waiting time stripping a frayed knot from *Mothwake*'s aft line while Nemi resisted the temptation to ask the sea what it was thinking.

Pell came down with a fresh pin for Ivo's wooden bird. He had heard their argument, or perhaps he had heard only his own portion repeated through the market.

“Better than the first one,” he said. “Marra sent it. You don't owe me a route in exchange.”

Ivo took the pin and tested the wings. “What do I owe you?”

“If you see a garden with the same hinge problem, tell them where to find a repair maker. Not where you sleep. I'll settle for that.”

It was a smaller agreement than either of them had tried to make in the workshop, and less fragile. Ivo tucked the bird into their coat beside the collar.

When Arlen returned, she did not say the passage was safe in any lasting sense. She described the checked approach as it stood, and Tava sounded their portion again. The two pilots compared the part where their observations met. The water was usable for *Mothwake*'s present draft and the retreat to Lacebank; the small-craft path had not become a channel for the inhabited ferry.

Tava looked back at the Rillturn quay. Eda stood there with an empty drawer, holding up the custody receipt so the ink could dry. Nemi waved to her. Marra waved back with a hand dark from the forge, and Ivo raised the wooden bird in answer.

The departure felt less daring than the arrival. Nemi knew now what losing the passage would cost: a real workshop, names, a collar, the promise of further work that might or might not be possible. Tava kept the cutter inside checked water while the island's low reef shifted in the distance. Arlen remained in her own observed approach until both boats could turn safely apart. Nemi could not tell when the sea stopped being familiar; only that one day they would tell the story of these few moments as though the entire world had held together for them.

At Lacebank, the pale stone gave them shelter again. Tava checked the last local leg toward *Kindling Tide* rather than assuming the home ferry would remain where it had been. A wash of sunlight broke across the moving island beneath the vessel's present mooring. The pear tree on the ferry's upper deck shone like a small lantern.

Liri was the first to see them. She screamed with joy before knowing whether the mission had succeeded. Pera caught her by the collar of her shirt, which was now held together with a badly tied knot, and told her to wait until the cutter was properly secured.

Ivo stepped onto the deck and held up the copper collar.

Pera did not cheer. She took it with both hands and inspected the seam, then inspected Ivo's face.

“One?”

“One,” Ivo said. “And this works with our sleeve. Marra showed me how to let the copper move without tearing the cloth.”

“What happened to the rest?”

“The families who needed them still need them.”

Pera nodded. She looked toward the herb bed they had considered dismantling. Its damaged edge had been held through the past days with borrowed wire; its leaves hung heavy and tired. Below it, the pear cradle still stood. The new collar could mend the leaking main trough. It could not replace all the worn fittings, and they would still have to choose where to take binding material for the others.

Liri reached for the shiny ring. “Is that ours?”

“It's yours if you carry it carefully,” Ivo said.

She did, with such intense solemnity that Nemi had to look away to keep from laughing. The collar passed around the supper table. Pera cooked herb bread in a shallow pan and waited while Ivo told the story of the workshop. She listened most carefully to the part where another maker had shown Ivo the weakness in the sample.

“So it was not perfect,” Pera said.

“No.”

“Good. Otherwise you'd have had nobody worth meeting.”

Nemi expected Ivo to be offended. Instead, they laughed with their mouth full of bread.

After the meal, Ivo and Pera went back to the upper deck. Marra's collar fitted the damaged trough, though it took both of them and Tava to hold the old edge while Ivo loosened the sleeve. Liri brought the cup of water that had once been used to test the repair, claiming it was the only cup that could properly judge the result.

They poured. Water gathered along the new loop, curved inward and struck the trough instead of the deck below. No one spoke until the third cup. Then Liri began singing the little rain-counting rhyme her grandmother had taught her, a song with too many verses because someone had added a new one whenever a year had been particularly wet.

Pera sang the wrong verse on purpose. Liri corrected her. Ivo's hands were red from pulling the cloth tight, and they looked more pleased with them than Nemi had ever seen.

The repaired trough did not solve the other leaks. That evening Pera announced that they would still dismantle part of the window bed for binding material. Liri wept at the news of the red flowers. Pera held her while she wept and explained that the roots could be taken up carefully, and that some of them might grow again. It was not a promise that all of them would.

Later, Nemi carried the sealed envelope through the cabin corridor. Its wax was chipped, its address still legible. They wrote its custody entry in the crew's ledger, in the dry cabinet whose drawers smelled of old salt and clean paper. They wrote the actual places: Rillturn, *Mothwake*, *Kindling Tide*. They wrote that it had not reached Larkhome.

There was room beneath the entry for another name.

Nemi left the space blank.

Outside, the household was settling. Someone was playing a slow tune on a flute. From the upper deck came the sound of Liri asking whether a transplanted flower could belong to two beds if the roots were kept together. Pera said she would ask the gardeners in the morning.

Tava appeared in the doorway, carrying the repaired sounding line.

“You look as though you brought back a burden,” they said.

“Two,” Nemi replied. “A collar for the ship, and a letter for people I don't know.”

“The collar is in the trough.”

“Yes.”

“The letter is in its place?”

“For now.”

Tava nodded. They had spent enough time on living water to know that *for now* could be the beginning of a good thing or the end of one.

Nemi closed the cabinet. Before they put out the lamp they could hear rain beginning on the gardens, light at first, then steadier. Above them one seam no longer dripped onto the deck. Another still did. The two sounds made an uneven rhythm, like an unfinished song that somebody, somewhere, might have the courage to continue.
