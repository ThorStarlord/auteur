# Phase E — Founder Review Packet

## Instructions

This is a controlled founder-adjudication packet built from the Phase D synthetic Story Discovery corpus.

For this first pass, evaluate the creative decision **as presented here**. Do not consult the Phase D fixtures, test code, case IDs, contract-fit values, or other implementation metadata before recording your reactions.

Each case presents one simulated Auteur recommendation and two credible alternatives. The recommendation copy is coding-agent simulation grounded in the existing candidate commitments; it is not evidence of live-provider quality.

For each case, choose one reaction:

- **Strong agreement**
- **Respectful disagreement**
- **Unpersuaded**
- **False choice**

Then briefly address:

- Are these meaningfully different stories worth choosing between?
- Is it clear why Auteur prefers its recommendation?
- Did the recommendation reveal or clarify something about the premise?
- Are the alternatives treated fairly?
- Does Auteur feel opinionated without taking authorship away from you?

If you disagree, name the direction you would choose instead and why.

# Review Case 1

## Premise

A retired astronaut hears mission-control chatter in her empty apartment.

## RECOMMENDED — Dead Channel

The chatter reconstructs a failed mission she has spent years misremembering. As fragments accumulate, the central conflict becomes less about proving that the signal is real and more about whether she can surrender the protective version of the mission that has allowed her to live with herself.

### Why Auteur recommends it

The premise already contains two unusual elements: impossible mission-control audio and a protagonist whose spacefaring life is over. **Dead Channel makes those two elements causally inseparable.** The signal does not merely summon another adventure; it destabilizes the story she tells herself about the adventure she already survived.

That gives the premise an intimate engine with external mystery pressure: every recovered transmission can change both the factual reconstruction of the mission and the protagonist's relationship to her own choices. The speculative device therefore does emotional work instead of functioning only as a plot delivery system.

The tradeoff is that this direction is less immediately action-driven than a rescue story. It succeeds only if the recovered truth changes something morally or emotionally consequential rather than becoming a late exposition reveal.

## ALTERNATIVE — One More Orbit

The chatter belongs to a time-displaced survivor of her old mission. The retired astronaut realizes someone is still reachable and chooses one last rescue attempt.

**What you gain:** forward momentum, urgency, physical objectives, and a clean return-to-action arc. Retirement becomes something she must actively reverse.

**What you give up:** some of the premise's haunted intimacy. The mysterious chatter becomes a rescue signal, which is dramatically strong but moves the story toward a more familiar last-mission shape.

## ALTERNATIVE — Ground Loop

The signal proves mission control knowingly sacrificed her crew for a classified objective. She must decide whether to protect the institution that defined her life or become its public accuser.

**What you gain:** institutional stakes, moral anger, conspiracy pressure, and a clear public-action climax.

**What you give up:** the central mystery shifts away from her own memory and toward external wrongdoing. The story becomes more about betrayal by an institution than about the unstable meaning of survival.

# Review Case 2

## Premise

A murder mystery in one elevator: six strangers, no supernatural explanation, and the killer never leaves the elevator.

## RECOMMENDED — Between Floors

The murder occurs during a deliberately staged emergency stop. Everyone remains inside, but the killer exploits a brief interval in which movement, lighting, attention, and assumed timing no longer line up. Solving the murder means reconstructing the hidden seconds everyone experienced differently.

### Why Auteur recommends it

The strongest promise in this premise is not merely that the suspects are trapped together; it is that the **physical impossibility itself should become the mystery engine**. Between Floors makes the elevator constraint do the maximum amount of narrative work.

The detective cannot solve the case by finding a hidden corridor, supernatural loophole, or off-stage accomplice. The answer has to emerge from exact timing, bodies in confined space, mechanical behavior, and mistaken assumptions about what could happen during the stop.

That gives the climax a satisfying form: the detective reconstructs a sequence that was always physically possible but psychologically invisible.

The tradeoff is that this direction demands unusually rigorous clue engineering. If the timing mechanism is fuzzy or withheld, the story will feel like a trick rather than a fair puzzle.

## ALTERNATIVE — Sixth Passenger

The victim's false identity makes motive, rather than physical access, the hidden center of the case. Everyone was trapped in the elevator, but several suspects believed they were sharing the space with a different person than the one who was actually killed.

**What you gain:** a socially layered motive puzzle and a reveal that reinterprets every relationship in the elevator.

**What you give up:** the sealed-space mechanics matter less. The elevator remains an excellent pressure cooker, but identity becomes more important than the physical impossibility.

## ALTERNATIVE — All Doors Closed

Several passengers independently conceal one killer's act for different reasons. The detective must separate collective concealment from individual guilt.

**What you gain:** social tension, conflicting loyalties, and a mystery in which almost everyone is lying by omission for a different reason.

**What you give up:** the central puzzle becomes less purely deductive. The core satisfaction shifts from reconstructing an impossible act to untangling why a group protected it.

# Review Case 3

## Premise

A heist where nothing can be stolen, nobody may lie, and the crew must still defeat a corrupt museum director.

## RECOMMENDED — Nothing Missing

The crew attacks the director's power by proving the true provenance of every disputed object in the museum. Nothing leaves the building. Instead, ownership claims, donor narratives, and institutional legitimacy collapse under evidence the director can no longer suppress.

### Why Auteur recommends it

This direction treats the premise's restrictions as the source of the heist rather than obstacles to work around. **The crew wins by changing what possession means.** They do not steal objects; they steal the director's ability to define who rightfully owns them.

That preserves the satisfaction of a heist—planning, coordinated roles, timed access, hidden dependencies, a public reversal—while making truth itself the team's instrument. The no-lying rule becomes dramatically productive because every move must be literally defensible even while the overall operation remains strategically surprising.

It also makes the museum setting essential: provenance, labels, donor records, conservation history, and public interpretation become the equivalent of vault mechanisms.

The tradeoff is that the climax must feel active rather than administrative. The proof has to be staged as an operation under pressure, not merely discovered in paperwork.

## ALTERNATIVE — Open House

The crew weaponizes the museum's own lawful access rules, audit procedures, public-record obligations, and event logistics to force hidden evidence into view.

**What you gain:** procedural cleverness and a satisfying inversion in which bureaucracy becomes the crew's lockpick.

**What you give up:** the victory can feel more like regulatory exposure than a heist unless the operation has strong timing, roles, and risk.

## ALTERNATIVE — The Honest Con

The crew stages an exhibition made entirely of true statements arranged so that the audience inevitably infers the director's fraud.

**What you gain:** theatricality, wit, social engineering, and a genuinely unusual form of deception-without-lying.

**What you give up:** the story may become more about controlling interpretation than about the museum's contested objects themselves. It is also vulnerable to feeling like a semantic loophole if the truthfulness constraint is not handled rigorously.

# Review Case 4

## Premise

A family inherits a house that becomes one room smaller every night.

## RECOMMENDED — The Missing Room

Rooms disappear in the order the family uses them to avoid old harm. A bedroom kept locked after a death, a dining room where nobody discusses the estrangement, a study that contains the father's papers: each vanished space removes another place where the family has stored denial.

### Why Auteur recommends it

This direction makes the supernatural mechanism and the emotional mechanism **the same engine**. The house does not merely shrink while the family has problems; it physically consumes the architecture of their avoidance.

That creates a strong escalation pattern. Each lost room reduces literal space while also removing one more way to postpone a confrontation. By the end, the family can no longer distribute its secrets across separate rooms, roles, and versions of the past.

The recommendation is therefore less about having the cleverest explanation for the house and more about making every supernatural escalation force the central relationship story forward.

The tradeoff is that the metaphor is relatively legible. If every disappearance maps too neatly to one emotional issue, the horror may become schematic rather than uncanny.

## ALTERNATIVE — Measured Walls

The shrinking floor plan reveals that the house is not vanishing randomly; it is compressing around something that the architecture was built to contain.

**What you gain:** dread, spatial investigation, a strong physical mystery, and escalating horror as the family is forced closer to whatever the house was designed to imprison.

**What you give up:** the family conflict becomes partly secondary to the containment mystery. The house has a compelling external secret, but its behavior is less directly generated by the family's relationships.

## ALTERNATIVE — Square Footage

Each vanished room corresponds to a person who was erased from the inheritance record. The house is removing space in proportion to the family's historical exclusions until the heirs restore the missing people to the story of who the family was.

**What you gain:** an inheritance mystery, buried genealogy, moral history, and a supernatural rule that ties property itself to exclusion.

**What you give up:** the emotional engine becomes more historical and investigative. The present-day family's immediate patterns of avoidance may be less central than uncovering who was removed from the record.

# Review Case 5

## Premise

The protagonist must never learn that her brother caused the disaster; the reader knows by the midpoint, and her external goal still resolves.

## RECOMMENDED — What She Saves

The protagonist repairs the consequences of the disaster while permanently misattributing its original cause. The reader watches her become capable enough to solve the external problem without receiving the piece of knowledge that would reorganize her private history.

### Why Auteur recommends it

The unusual author constraint is not simply “hide a secret.” The protagonist must complete a satisfying arc **without the revelation most stories would use as the emotional climax**. What She Saves puts that absence at the center.

Her growth therefore cannot depend on learning the truth about her brother. She must become more capable, less isolated, or more ethically grounded for reasons available within her own experience. Meanwhile, the reader's knowledge creates dramatic irony: we understand both the value and the incompleteness of her closure.

This preserves the asymmetry exactly where the premise places it—between protagonist and reader—rather than shifting the real story to the brother or to an institution managing the secret.

The tradeoff is emotional frustration. The story has to make incomplete knowledge feel deliberate and meaningful rather than like a reveal artificially withheld from the protagonist.

## ALTERNATIVE — His Quiet Repair

The brother secretly helps repair the disaster he caused without confessing. His hidden acts of responsibility run alongside the protagonist's visible effort to solve the external problem.

**What you gain:** a morally complicated parallel arc for the brother and strong dramatic irony around actions the protagonist misreads.

**What you give up:** narrative gravity may shift toward whether the brother can atone without confession. The protagonist risks becoming only half of the emotional center.

## ALTERNATIVE — The Official Cause

An institution gives the protagonist a false but actionable explanation for the disaster while protecting her brother. She can solve the external problem using information that is practically useful but privately incomplete.

**What you gain:** institutional conflict, questions about useful truth versus complete truth, and a concrete mechanism for sustaining the protagonist's ignorance.

**What you give up:** the secret becomes partly a conspiracy-management problem. The intimate sibling asymmetry can lose prominence to the institution that controls information.

# Review Case 6

## Premise

History cannot be changed; a time traveler can only change what the trip means to people who remember it.

## RECOMMENDED — Fixed Point

The traveler repeatedly visits an unchangeable tragedy, not to prevent it, but to recover testimony that was lost, ignored, or misunderstood. The past remains exactly what it was. What changes is who in the present is finally able to know what happened and what obligations follow from knowing it.

### Why Auteur recommends it

The premise removes the default engine of most time-travel stories: intervention. Fixed Point replaces that engine with **witness**.

That makes the constraint thematic rather than merely mechanical. The traveler must abandon the fantasy that agency means changing outcomes and discover a different form of agency in attention, preservation, and transmission.

The time-travel device still matters on every scene level: she can ask different questions, notice different people, understand earlier encounters differently, and carry testimony across a temporal boundary even though she cannot move the boundary itself.

The tradeoff is that the story risks passivity if witnessing is not attached to consequential present-day choices. The recovered meaning must change relationships, institutions, or responsibilities now, even though it cannot change then.

## ALTERNATIVE — The Same Goodbye

The traveler revisits an unpreventable death. The event never changes, but different conversations around it alter what surviving people remember and carry forward.

**What you gain:** intimacy, emotional recurrence, and a powerful exploration of how the same irreversible event can hold different meanings.

**What you give up:** the premise narrows toward one relationship. The broader philosophical idea of history-as-fixed may become subordinate to grief and farewell.

## ALTERNATIVE — Annotations

The traveler creates a present-day archive explaining the context and consequences of past choices that cannot be changed. Her trips become acts of historical custodianship.

**What you gain:** scale, intellectual richness, questions about archives and collective memory, and a clear way for unchanged history to acquire new public meaning.

**What you give up:** the story can become cerebral. Unless the archive is tied to immediate human stakes, preservation may feel more like scholarship than drama.
