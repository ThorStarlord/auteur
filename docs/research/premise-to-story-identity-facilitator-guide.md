# Premise-to-StoryIdentity Facilitator Guide

Protocol companion for the [Premise-to-StoryIdentity Research
Experiment](premise-to-story-identity-experiment.md).

This guide is for a 45–60 minute, one-on-one, moderated concept-test session.
It tests whether a creative beginner can understand and act on a proposed
whole-story direction. It does not test a product surface, invoke Auteur's
recommendation command, or create canonical project artifacts.

## Facilitator invariants

Keep these boundaries visible throughout the session:

- A proposed `StoryIdentity` is a recommendation, not accepted canon.
- The participant may accept, reject, or revise the proposal.
- A rejection or revision is valid research evidence, not a failed creative
  outcome.
- Do not silently change the participant's premise or commitments.
- Do not use the five semantic layers as a teaching sequence.
- Do not introduce chapter planning, prose drafting, or structural diagnosis.
- Do not choose or imply a browser, TUI, editor, or CLI surface.

## Participant screening

Include participants who:

- identify as inexperienced or relatively inexperienced with long-form
  narrative planning;
- have a premise, character impulse, or desired reader experience to bring;
- can participate in a one-on-one session of approximately 45–60 minutes;
- are willing to explain their understanding and decisions aloud.

Do not screen for technical ability. Record it only as context if the
participant volunteers it.

Before the session, assign a participant code such as `P-01`. Do not place a
participant's name, contact information, or raw premise in repository files.

## Consent and privacy

Before beginning, explain in plain language:

- the session evaluates the proposed experience, not the participant's
  creative ability;
- participation is voluntary and they may skip a question or stop;
- the facilitator will ask them to explain what they think the proposal means;
- notes will separate their words from researcher interpretation;
- only anonymized aggregate findings will be stored in the repository;
- private raw notes and any recording are stored outside the repository and
  handled under the approved research process.

Obtain the applicable informed consent before collecting research data. Do not
record audio or video unless the participant has explicitly consented through
that process.

## Materials

Prepare these materials before each session:

1. This guide at the frozen protocol version.
2. A private evidence-capture sheet using the template below.
3. A premise-reflection card.
4. A manually prepared proposal packet using the fixed template below.
5. A timer.

The proposal packet must be prepared from the participant's premise. Do not
invoke `auteur identity recommend` for the study.

## Session script

The timings are guidance, not a reason to rush a participant who is thinking.

### 1. Welcome and consent — 0–5 minutes

Say:

> We are testing whether this proposed story-planning experience is clear and
> useful. We are not evaluating your writing ability. You can reject or change
> anything you see, and you can stop at any time.

Confirm consent and explain that the proposal is provisional until they make an
explicit decision.

### 2. Premise intake — 5–10 minutes

Ask:

> What story idea, character impulse, or reader experience would you like to
> explore today?

Capture the response verbatim in private notes. If the participant cannot
provide an input, use the study's standardized fallback and mark that choice.

Do not ask them to complete an outline or fill in missing narrative machinery.

### 2a. Bounded-complexity triage — before the reflection

Use this step when the premise contains multiple important threads, dense
setting material, or explicit constraints that could compete with one another.
The v1.2 stress protocol uses it before presenting a proposed `StoryIdentity`.

Show a concise premise inventory containing:

- important goals or threads;
- explicit constraints;
- unresolved questions; and
- context that may be needed for the current Identity decision.

Then show one recommended primary Book-level thread and say:

> Auteur recommends this as the primary direction for the Book. This is still
> a proposal. What would you accept, revise, or leave unresolved about this
> recommendation?

Record the participant's response. Classify other threads only as proposed
supporting, deferred, or unresolved material. Do not intentionally exclude a
thread without explicit participant approval.

Show the participant what context is deferred and ask:

> Which of these details do you need before deciding what this story is mainly
> about, and which can remain open for later?

For each important premise intention, show the compact preservation map:

| Premise intention | Proposed role | Representation or open decision |
|---|---|---|
| supplied goal or thread | primary/supporting/deferred/unresolved | where it appears |
| explicit constraint | preserved commitment | observable must/must-not behavior |
| unresolved question | open | what remains undecided |

Ask whether the map preserves the participant's intent. Do not silently repair
the map. Translate abstract constraints into observable commitments before
continuing; for example, explain what a character may choose, what they must
not be forced to do, and what would violate the constraint.

If the participant cannot identify a primary Book-level thread, pause the
session for clarification rather than forcing a `StoryIdentity` proposal. A
thoughtful triage pause is evidence about the unresolved decision, not a reason
to invent a direction for the participant.

### 3. Plain-language reflection — 10–15 minutes

Show the premise reflection card and say:

> Here is what we understood from your premise. What stands out as accurate,
> inaccurate, or missing?

Record corrections. Do not silently repair the reflection. If the participant
adds an authorial commitment, add it to the proposal input record and mark it as
participant-provided.

### 4. Recommended direction — 15–25 minutes

Show one recommended story direction, its rationale, and meaningful rejected
alternatives. Say:

> This is one proposed direction for the story. It is a recommendation, not a
> decision that has already been made. What do you think this direction means?

Use only neutral prompts if the participant hesitates:

- “What do you think this means?”
- “What feels clear?”
- “What feels uncertain?”
- “What part of your premise does this seem to preserve?”

Do not explain the narrative reasoning for them or steer them toward acceptance.

### 5. Proposed StoryIdentity — 25–32 minutes

Show the compact proposal packet. Say:

> This is the proposed Story Identity: the commitments that would define what
> this story is if you chose to accept them. Please explain the main commitments
> in your own words.

Listen for whether the participant can explain:

- intended reader experience and genre/subgenre promise;
- protagonist want and central resistance;
- conflict, stakes, change, and broad ending shape;
- what is still proposed rather than accepted;
- what they would preserve or revise.

Do not test whether they can memorize or pronounce canonical terms.

### 6. Explicit decision — 32–40 minutes

Ask:

> What would you like to do with this proposal: accept it, reject it, or revise
> it?

Then ask:

> What is the main reason for that decision?

Record the decision and rationale. Acceptance is not the desired answer; an
informed rejection or revision is equally useful.

### 7. One revision loop, if requested — 40–48 minutes

Ask:

> Which commitment would you like to change, and what would you like it to do
> instead?

Restate the request without adding interpretation:

> I heard you asking to change [commitment] from [current meaning] to [desired
> meaning]. Is that accurate?

Prepare and show a revised proposal with the original and revised commitments
visible side by side. Say:

> This revision is still a proposal. Would you accept this revised direction,
> reject it, or leave it provisional?

Do not silently apply a revision or overwrite the original proposal. Stop after
one revision loop and record any further desired changes as future research
evidence.

### 8. Next decision and close — 48–60 minutes

Ask:

> What is the next useful creative decision you would want to make for this
> story?

Then ask:

> Which parts of the direction feel decided, and which parts still feel open?

Optionally collect a brief confidence rating and ask what, if anything, made
the process difficult. Thank the participant and stop the research task.

## Classification rules

Classify a participant as `SUCCESS` only when they reach an explicit decision,
explain the main commitments, distinguish proposed from accepted commitments,
identify something to preserve or revise, and identify the next useful creative
decision without substantive coaching.

Classify as `FAILURE` when the participant cannot explain the direction after
one neutral clarification, mistakes a proposal for canon, cannot identify the
decision, loses premise intent without noticing, requires facilitator rescue,
or abandons because the concepts or authority boundary remain unclear.

Use `INCONCLUSIVE` when the session is interrupted, the fallback or protocol
deviation prevents a clear interpretation, or evidence is insufficient to
classify the result. Explain the reason rather than treating inconclusive as
success or failure.

## Stopping and versioning

- Run at least five sessions.
- Pause after two consecutive critical failures of the same kind.
- Stop at five if at least four participants succeed and the final two contain
  no critical failure.
- Continue to a maximum of eight when results are mixed or new failure modes
  appear.
- After eight, classify the experiment as failed or inconclusive if the success
  threshold is unmet.

Freeze this guide and the proposal-packet template before session one. If the
protocol changes after a critical failure, increment the protocol version and
label all evidence with that version. Do not pool materially different
protocols without documenting the difference.

The v1.2 bounded-complexity extension is documented in the
[stress simulation protocol](premise-to-story-identity-stress-simulation.md)
and its [findings report](premise-to-story-identity-stress-simulation-findings.md).
The v1.2 synthetic gate does not count as human participant evidence. A later
founder self-study must use `Record type: SELF_STUDY`, participant count `1`,
and explicitly record the researcher/participant overlap.
