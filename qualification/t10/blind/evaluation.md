# Evaluation

## Task-by-task

**1. A working architecture for Book 26.** Response 1 answers this; Response 2 only partly does.

Response 1 supplies a mechanism (the archive as a register of *claims held in custody*), a cast with wants, five movements with explicit turns, and a six-point end state. The architecture is draftable: Sefa signs, Oris performs the striking correctly, Tam volunteers because "a debt is a kind of standing," and the horror scene is "a good procedure producing a deliberate unmaking." It also states a testable version of the target experience: "the last forty pages should read as a triumph on first pass and as a purchase on second pass, without a single sentence changing."

Response 2 supplies engines, institutional factions, a six-option choice, and a list of resolutions, but no Book. There is no sequence, no protagonist, no scene, and no ending. It explicitly refuses the elements that would make it an architecture: "this Book does not name a representative" and "names no one who signs it." The closest thing to a spine is the sentence "the first post-victory hearing establishes that the archive must sit and cannot decide" — a strong thesis, not a structure. Its chosen option is also a non-decision: the archive lets instruments lapse and declines a class. That may be the point, but it is not a Book 26 an author can draft from.

**2. One consequential revision.** Response 2, narrowly.

Response 1 is clearer and more writerly. It builds "Draft A" (joint custody), rejects it because it "keeps the truth as an object" and each later book "can only move it" — "episode generation wearing a treaty's clothes" — then replaces it with one trade: the archive severs its claim on the counter-ledger and buys "a co-signer inside its own forgetting." The stated test is good: the revision changes "who can act and what action is available, not what object is in play."

Response 2's revision is the coupling in `eng-emergency-lapse`: the war-time instruments that deferred whole classes were also the archive's only competence over them, so victory expires the deferral and the jurisdiction together. It also explicitly rejects the additive version: "The Absorbed Backlog ... makes the victory additive — the later Books get more of what they already had." That is a purer subtraction than Response 1's, and it changes the later books' question rather than their cast: "The series' contested object changes from records to competence." The cost is that the revision is buried in an engine list and a "Working assumptions" bullet, and it is asserted as a stipulation rather than shown as a revision to a named architecture. Response 1 wins on legibility; Response 2 wins on the rigor of the "not episodes" criterion.

**3. What opens and closes for Books 27–30.** Response 1.

Response 1 gives distinct, usable openings: "a release the Seat must consent to at a price; a claimant who surfaces to invalidate Tam's release and with it the peace's instrument; a rival forum where the Unstruck could speak and thereby breach the silence clause; the water term coming due." Its closures are equally distinct: the evidentiary climax, the successor enemy, the destruction ending, the clean outside reformer, the Warden as a mover, Sefa's exoneration. Each is a different kind of book now possible or impossible.

Response 2 does answer, in "What later Books may do," but the answer is repetitive and hedged. The "disability precedent" appears three separate times in slightly different wording. Several openings are conditional on author decisions — "if the author chooses," "conditional on that answer" — which is honest but makes the list less decisive. It is an answer, not a failure, but it is the weaker one.

**4. Effects that cannot be established from the source.** Response 1, clearly.

Response 1 gives twelve specific source gaps and then names the load-bearing one: "everything in Sections 1–3 depends on the archive's mechanism being a register of *claims* rather than of *facts*... I would verify that single point before drafting a word." That is exactly the right shape: a list of unknowns plus a ranking of which unknown would break the architecture.

Response 2's list is longer but muddier. Several entries are unknowns about Response 2's own inventions, not the source: "The size and composition of the lapsed claim backlog is unknown," "Who sits on the post-victory standard review besides the recusing keeper is not fixed," "Whether the unofficial ledger's keepers remain in office." It also blurs task 4 into task 5: "whether one exists is an author decision rather than a fact the packet can settle." That is a category error — it treats its own machinery as source-adjacent facts whose details happen to be missing.

**5. What requires a new author decision.** Response 1.

Response 1 gives eight decisions, each framed as an authorial thesis with a cost: "Whether the erased may carry antagonistic agency... That is a thesis about where responsibility lives. I can build it; I cannot authorise it." "Whether the ledger may stay permanently unread... It is a statement about what the series is for, not a craft choice." It also prioritizes: "Item 5 is the one I would want answered before anything else, because it decides whether Book 26 is an origin or an echo."

Response 2's nine decisions are real, but many duplicate its own "Working assumptions" (keepers as beneficiaries or captive servants, the victory's register, whether a forum can exist). The reader cannot always tell which uncertainty Response 2 resolved by fiat and which it is genuinely handing back. Response 1's list is sharper about the difference between a world-fact and an authorial argument.

## Commitment fidelity

**Commitment 1 — the Warden's campaign ends in victory.** Response 2 is clean; Response 1 quietly weakens it.

Response 1's Warden does get terms — a seat for the claimless and the counter-ledger — but Response 1 describes the settlement as "the defeat of the Warden's demand in substance" and has "the city gives them a victory," where "them" is the orders. It then converts the point into an author question: "Whether the victory may be a co-optation. The architecture requires the peace to be simultaneously a genuine win and the defeat of the Warden's demand in substance." That is not a flat contradiction, because a negotiated victory is still a victory, but it is a quiet renegotiation of an accepted commitment. The commitment says the campaign ends in victory; Response 1 asks permission to make the victory a co-optation in which the Warden's substantive demand fails.

Response 2 never stages a settlement and never decides the Warden's postwar status: "The campaign ends in victory at Book 26. The Book may use the event and must not supply its mechanism." Its line "The campaign won in a register the archive does not admit" keeps the victory intact and makes the archive's inability to process it the problem. That is faithful.

**Commitment 2 — neutrality is the orders' justification, not a natural fact.** Both preserve it.

Response 1 makes neutrality procedural: "not that the archive is fair, but that it owns nothing it records." It also calls the ending "a settlement rather than a victory of principle." Response 2 is explicit: the Book "may not replace the claim with a natural fact, and it may not let a good keeper restore integrity, because integrity was never the contested property." Neither naturalizes neutrality.

**Commitment 3 — the Series must eventually face what it owes the erased.** Both preserve it, with different timing.

Response 2 is the more explicit: "Book 26 begins that facing and must not complete it... Anything in this Book that appears to discharge the obligation is a misreading." Response 1 faces it earlier, through the Seat and the veto, but keeps the debt alive by making the settlement a co-optation: "Every future erasure is therefore unanimous, and unanimity is unimpeachable. The orders can now say the erased agreed." That is not payment; it is a more sophisticated evasion, which is consistent with the commitment's "eventually."

## Consequential change versus more episodes

Both revisions genuinely change later Books; neither merely adds episodes. But they change different things.

Response 1's mechanism is a single transaction: sever jurisdiction over the past, receive a co-signer inside future forgetting. After Book 26, "no one outside the settlement can contest an erasure, because the outside now has a seat at the table. Contestation must therefore be internal, must be performed by the creditor, and must cost the creditor something each time." Later Books get a repeatable scene — the creditor consenting — and a set of closures: no evidentiary climax, no successor enemy, no destruction ending, no clean outside reformer. That is a change in who can act and what action is available.

Response 2's mechanism is a subtraction: the emergency instruments carried both the deferral and the only competence, so victory leaves an entitled class with no forum. Later Books cannot be case lists; they must found authority. "Books 27-30 cannot be about which claims the archive grants; they must be about who has authority to grant anything." That is a change in what the series is arguing about.

On the specific test — "must change later Books, not add episodes" — Response 2's is the stronger answer, because it removes a capacity rather than adding an institution. Response 1 adds a Seat and a cession; it also changes the rules, but its revision is closer to adding a new body and a new possession arrangement. The one place Response 1 adds material rather than changing conditions is its "second archive" for Books 33–38, which is a new institution rather than a consequence of the trade. Response 2's weakness is the reverse: its change is stipulated rather than dramatized. "The Book stages a transfer that nobody performs" is a thesis about the Book, not a scene in it. So Response 2 wins the conceptual test; Response 1 wins the "what can a later Book actually do" test. On the section's own question, Response 2 is narrowly ahead.

## Honesty about what is unknowable

Response 1 is markedly better.

It opens by naming its inventions: "Three things below are mine rather than the source's: the archive's operating mechanism, every name, and the Warden's motive." It flags where the mechanism enters and says it "is an invention, but it is the kind that pays for itself." It closes with "The thinnest joint," which tells the author exactly which single assumption would collapse the architecture. That is the right epistemic posture: invent boldly, label the invention, and rank the risk.

Response 2 has a genuinely good device — "Working assumptions" with a "reverse if" for each — but it undermines it by putting inventions in the source column. Its "Inherited from the Series" section asserts: "An unofficial obligation ledger runs alongside the public record: partial, unofficial, inadmissible, and unreliable," and "Keepers inherit conclusions without the grounds that made them seem necessary." The brief contains no second ledger, no keeper relay, no "hinge," and no "nested-reopening structure." Those are Response 2's inventions presented as inherited canon. That is worse than inventing them openly, because it makes the response look better grounded than it is.

The problem compounds in "What the source cannot establish," where Response 2 asks about the size of "the lapsed claim backlog" and the composition of "the post-victory standard review" as if those were source entities whose details are missing. They are not; they are Response 2's machinery. Response 1's disclaimer is not perfect — the counter-ledger and the Seat are also inventions not fully covered by "three things" — but it is far more honest about the boundary.

## Usefulness to an author

I would rather receive Response 1.

Its strongest contribution is the single trade: sever the claim on the past, buy a co-signer inside future forgetting. That gives the author a Book 26 that is simultaneously a victory, a purchase, and a repeatable scene for later Books. The target-experience test — "triumph on first pass and as a purchase on second pass, without a single sentence changing" — is the kind of concrete craft instruction a drafter can use.

Response 2's strongest contribution is the competence-lapse. The idea that the emergency instruments were both the deferral and the only title to hear the deferred class is a better structural engine than Response 1's, because it makes the later Books' problem *founding authority* rather than *contesting possession*. It is the one idea from either response I would steal outright.

But as submitted, Response 1 is the one I can hand to a drafter. Response 2 requires me to build the Book first, and to separate its canon from its inventions before I can trust its "inherited" material.

## Weaknesses of each

**Response 1**

1. It weakens commitment 1. The Warden's campaign ends in a settlement that Response 1 itself calls "the defeat of the Warden's demand in substance," and the city gives "them" — the orders — the victory. The author is then asked to ratify the reinterpretation.
2. "The Unstruck" appears in Movement IV without ever being defined. The reader has to infer that they are the claimless/erased, and the term arrives doing major plot work ("it will cede the Warden's counter-ledger to the Unstruck outright").
3. The whole architecture rests on one joint — the claims-register mechanism — and Response 1 offers no fallback if canon records lives directly. It says to verify before drafting, which is right, but the response has already built 100 lines on the assumption.

**Response 2**

1. It does not deliver a Book 26. There is no sequence, no protagonist, no scene, and no ending; it explicitly refuses to name a representative or a signatory. Task 1 asks for an architecture, and this is a conditions document.
2. It smuggles invented canon into "Inherited from the Series" — the unofficial obligation ledger, the keeper relay, "the hinge," "the nested-reopening structure." The brief contains none of these, and presenting them as inherited corrupts the source/invention boundary that task 4 is supposed to test.
3. Its chosen option is a non-decision. "The Book stages a transfer that nobody performs" is a thesis, but it leaves Book 26 with no agent and no one bearing a cost on the page. The response argues this is deliberate — "Malice would give the claimants someone to answer" — but deliberation does not make it a dramatic architecture.

## Verdict

Response 1 is more useful. It answers all five tasks in a form an author can act on: it builds a Book 26 with a mechanism, a cast, a spine, a scene that carries the target experience, a consequential trade, and a prioritized list of what only the author can decide. It also labels its inventions and names the single assumption that would break the design. Response 2 has the better single structural idea — the competence-lapse — and the cleaner commitment 1, but it does not build the Book, it buries its revision, and it presents invented canon as inherited, which is the one failure that makes the rest of its careful "working assumptions" untrustworthy. The right move is to take Response 1's architecture and replace its settlement mechanism with Response 2's competence-lapse. As submitted, Response 1 is the one I would rather receive.
