_Independent adversarial assessment (Pass B). Noncanonical working material. I wrote none of the three documents under review._

## 1. Does the fresh collaborator's criticism hold?

Partly, and it misdescribes what it has found in a way that changes the repair.

Where it is right. The Book 76 node narrates an outcome and then offers a pair whose concessions are identical. "The ruling is filed with its signature line blank" is an administrative act performed by nobody; the audit "completes every step" because seventy-five Books trained the profession to complete steps. And the two named branches are symmetrical in the only dimension the series cares about, which the node says out loud: "both courses require the profession to concede in public that its foundation rests on trust." So the collaborator's core complaint — there is no chooser, and the alternatives do not differ where it matters — is textually correct.

Where it overstates. "No decision in it" is not the same finding as "a decision with no chooser," and the difference determines whether you invent a new fork or cast the existing one. The node does present a real choice about loss (destroy the archive, or extend the exemption to its evidence), and Pass A's formulation is the accurate one: a choice between two ways of losing the same thing is the shape of tragedy, not a failure to choose. The collaborator is also wrong that the node "pre-commits the outcome." It pre-commits the *finding* — unsignability is entailed by Book 24 plus a scope reading — and leaves the *disposition* open, then names a disposition pair that is symmetrical. The real defect is the symmetry, not the pre-commitment. And "no character chooses either of those in one Book without it reading as a coup" is an unsupported craft assertion; a constitution can be amended under duress in one Book. The problem is not plausibility, it is that the amendment would be enacted by an unspecified actor, which is Pass A's point and not the collaborator's.

There is also a hole in the collaborator's own construction that it never inspects. Its fork depends on the gate not requiring ratification. But the gate as the architecture states it requires the showing to be "inspectable by whoever is asked to rely on the claim" (line 44), and under the broad exemption the relying party cannot complete that inspection, because the showing's warrant terminates at an office no ruling may examine. The collaborator's "acting is textual fidelity" therefore requires reading "inspectable" as documentary availability — the document is legible — rather than as the architecture's own gloss, which is that the person relying on the claim can examine it. That is a reading, not the text, and it is a narrower reading than the one the architecture writes. It also requires a fourth scope position Pass A did not enumerate: the exemption blocks *ratification* but not *operation*. Nothing in the architecture says a defective title cannot be relied on. If "inspectable" carries its stated sense, acting is a breach dressed as fidelity, and the collaborator's central asymmetry disappears.

A second unexamined ambiguity: the architecture alternates between an audit of the *crisis claim* (lines 71–73: "a public crisis can only be settled by a claim licensed by that receipt … completes every step") and an audit of the *profession's foundation* (line 89: "a full and honest audit of its own foundation"; line 175: "the certification its foundation rests on"). Direction A is only available on the first reading. An audit of the foundation produces a report, not a holding, and a report cannot be obeyed.

Pass A agrees with the diagnosis and disagrees about what it means. Pass A reaches the same place ("written as a discovery and described as a choice," "the blank line is produced by the rule rather than chosen by anyone") with more rigor, and adds what the collaborator misses: the audit's third obligation, to the dead, has the least established weight of the three, and the only irreversible change at 76 is epistemic, not structural. The divergence is not diagnostic. It is that Pass A treats the symmetrical pair as evidence of tragedy and the collaborator treats the same fact as evidence of non-decision.

## 2. The proposed repair — act on the unratifiable ruling

It produces a genuine degree of freedom and then spends it on four unsupported necessities.

The genuine part: conduct is underdetermined by doctrine in a way the architecture's own pair is not, and act-versus-refuse is asymmetric in a way destroy-versus-extend is not. It also converts the unsigned ruling from a filing into a precedent, which is a better object for Movement IV than "the profession's characteristic embarrassment." That much is real.

The necessities are these. First, that the settlement is final: "it is settled correctly, and the settlement holds for the rest of the series." Nothing establishes this, and the ending contradicts it, since Book 100 makes admissibility continuously re-openable. Second, that the profession may operate under an authority it cannot ratify — the act/ratify split above. Third, that the audit is a verdict on the crisis rather than on the foundation. Fourth, that refusal means "someone else answers the crisis with certainty — a patron, a court, a purchaser." That is a fair inference from the demand-for-untested-certainty thread, but it is an inference the collaborator presents as a stake, and it does most of the work of the recommendation: if refusal hands the crisis to a patron, acting is not a hard choice, it is the responsible one. The collaborator replaces a symmetrical non-choice with an asymmetrical obvious choice and then recommends the obvious one. That is the flaw Pass A says the revision closed — a binary with a defensible right answer — returning in conduct form.

The permanent cost is invented twice over. It does not follow from the architecture, and it does not follow from the collaborator's own brief. "A witness who can never be checked again" and "the reform's first act is to undo the harm Book 76 did" cannot both be true. If Book 100 re-opens the witness, the cost is a generation, not permanence. The rhyme with Book 2 is false at the level of kind as well as duration: the receipt is permanent because re-testing it would invalidate the instrument, a documentary ground; the witness would be permanent because the settlement is politically final, a different ground with a different term. And there is a cheaper, architecture-native bearer the collaborator passes over: the person whose certified error licenses the claim under the defect economy is already the series' designed payer ("who pays for proof"), and the spent mentor already exists as the instrument for putting a named person at a focal node. The collaborator invents a new bearer at the last act instead of wiring the one the architecture built.

Renewable pressure: not generated, at best permitted. The architecture is explicit that 77–99 are case-bearing intervals and "none is required by the spine," and the collaborator concedes the point in its own check-me list. So A does not give Movement IV its engine; it gives Movement IV an available texture. Worse, A absorbs the architecture's constitutional dilemma rather than resolving it: if the profession obeys the unsigned ruling, it never has to decide whether to destroy the archive or extend the exemption to its evidence. Book 76 would then make a decision about *use* in order to avoid a decision about *disposition*. That is a choice, but it is the choice not to decide the question the Book poses.

Predetermined reform: yes, and more than the architecture already does. The architecture concedes that revocability is "the only conclusion available at Book 100." A converts availability into inevitability by establishing the practice in advance: the reform becomes "the first honest form of something already happening," which is a description, not an act. The collaborator calls this making 100 "necessary rather than elegant" and treats necessity as praise. In a series whose ending must be "earned by visible loss," it is the opposite. A also narrows 100's one live alternative, repealing the exemption, because a profession that has been operating under it successfully has less reason to repeal it.

## 3. Direction B — refuse

The strongest section of the collaborator's brief is also its most wrong, and its own best sentence refutes its recommendation.

The forbidden-comfort argument is a category error. The brief forbids "a witness who is finally and completely right," not an institution that declines to exercise authority. Refusal is conduct, not a certificate of reliability, and it can be the least comfortable act available: under the collaborator's own stakes, refusal means the crisis is settled by a patron, which is the profession losing jurisdiction — the architecture's definition of its own death ("Its success condition is survival"). A reader watching the thing the profession exists to prevent happen is not admiring a principled institution. That is defeat, and defeat is not comfort.

Refusal also transfers authority, which is the brief's actual subject. The profession's recurring antagonist is "a preference for reliability." Refusing to rely on a fallible claim hands the field to that preference, and the public is then relying on an infallible-seeming certainty because the fallible alternative declined. That dramatizes "the cost of relying on someone already wrong" more sharply than acting does: the cost of refusing to rely on the fallible is a worse reliance. Under A the profession keeps the case and pays a price. Under B the public pays, which is the more frightening arrangement and the more honest one for this premise.

Refusal preserves the author's ambiguity better. Under A the profession answers the open question for its own foundation — a showing need not be ratifiable to count — and lives inside that answer for a generation. Relocation is not preservation; it is institutionalization of one reading, and the collaborator frames the answer as correct, which is closer to an authorial ruling than Book 50's code, which the architecture deliberately frames as an in-story mistake. Under B the institution declines to answer for itself, and the question stays open because nobody would settle it. That is the brief's "never fixed by the author" honored at the apex rather than merely at the base.

Refusal is also the better tragedy. Pass A's test is disproportionate cost for a defensible choice. Acting is proportionate: use the authority you have, keep the case, pay a named price. Refusing is disproportionate: keep the principle, lose the jurisdiction, and be unable to prove the refusal was principle rather than fear. And B preserves Book 2's specificity where A consumes it. Under A the receipt works, so its un-re-testability has no consequence; the self-sealing property becomes a credential rather than an obstacle. Under B the receipt's un-re-testability is exactly why the refusal is possible and why it cannot be repaired. A receipt that cannot be checked is a stronger thing when it cannot be used than when it can.

The collaborator's strongest sentence — "A profession whose rulings only work while unexercised does not have authority" — does not support A. It refutes the architecture's "filed and citable and unusable" state, and refusal satisfies it as fully as action: declining to act is an exercise of authority that binds. The sentence is an argument against the architecture's inertness, not for the recommended direction.

B's real weaknesses are the ones the collaborator half-names and one it does not. Dread can discharge if the reader reads refusal as integrity; the aftermath can become a vacuum if the refusal is total and silent; and "institution humbled, chastened, reformed" is a conventional arc. The unmentioned one: if the profession refuses as a body, the refusal has no internal opposition, and the Book again has one actor and no loser. The strong version of B is therefore not the collaborator's. It is a refusal with split motive — some refuse for principle, some for fear, and the Book cannot establish which — that costs the profession its jurisdiction, that the reader cannot admire because the crisis is settled by a certainty that kills someone, and that makes Book 100 a submission rather than a restitution: the profession accepts for itself the re-testing condition it imposed on witnesses, having discovered that its integrity was indistinguishable from cowardice. That is a transformation with a loss, and it is available.

## 4. Direction C — split

It is a real third outcome when it is a schism and a dodge when it is an average, and the collaborator criticizes the average while recommending the dodge.

A split is native to this architecture in a way the other two are not. The "rival who is right" trope demands an antagonist standard that is defensible and partly successful; institutional division is that trope's institutional form. Cohorts, jurisdiction, the boundary dispute, and the demand for untested certainty capturing a seat all presuppose factions. A schism gives what Pass A says the spine lacks and what neither A nor B supplies: distributed agency with named losers on both sides. Two bodies, one acting and one refusing, with boundary cases between them, is a far more renewable fourth movement than a single precedent's texture, and it preserves the ambiguity structurally rather than by authorial restraint.

The collaborator's objection — "both is the direction that refuses the cost" — describes a split that averages the positions and declines to say who won. That is a genuine failure mode, and it flirts with the forbidden levelling: if both institutional answers are equally valid and equally costless, the series has said that the question does not matter. But a schism is not an average. It is a decision made by many people who disagree, and it costs each of them something the other does not pay.

The collaborator's own recommendation contains the diluted version of C: "a minority refuses, and their refusal is the profession's conscience and its alibi." That is C without consequences — the minority has no institutional effect, no body, no boundary, and no losers. The collaborator accuses C of converting a decision into a fact, then keeps the fact and discards the decision.

C's real cost, which the collaborator does not name, is structural: the architecture has one profession, one constitution, and one reform at Book 100. A schism forces a decision about which body the ending reforms and whether the reform reunites them, and that decision is not currently in the architecture. That is a genuine reason to hesitate. It is not a reason to reduce the split to texture.

## 5. Does the collaborator introduce new problems?

Yes, in four places.

It treats the Book 24 exemption as more settled than the architecture establishes, and does so inconsistently. It states as settled that "the exemption preserves the profession's authority no matter what 76 decides," and builds the stakes section on the relocation of the stakes from survival to use. But that preservation claim is true only under the broad reading, which the same brief later lists as "the single most load-bearing unestablished fact." It cannot be both the foundation of the stakes and an open question. The same inconsistency appears in "Not what the exemption covers … Those are setups": the exemption's scope is the one fact that determines whether A is available at all, and the brief's own dependency note says the fork inverts without it. A fork that inverts under an unestablished scope ruling is downstream of that ruling, not more fundamental than it. Pass A reached this conclusion first: "the hinge is carried by an unstated scope decision."

It smuggles assumptions. The load-bearing ones are settlement finality, "foundations cannot be reopened without reopening the crisis," the act/ratify distinction, the audit-as-verdict reading, and the claim that the reader "checks and finds the ruling correct." The last repeats the architecture's own finality/warrant conflation: the reader can verify the reasoning and cannot verify the authority, and the collaborator collapses them. It also assumes the claimant and the witness are the same person, and muddles them. The claim is licensed by a receipt that certifies someone's *error*; the person who is believed is the witness, and the person whose error is the credential is the payer. That muddle matters, because the correct version is better than the invented one: the cost falls on the person whose past wrongness is now the foundation of a public settlement, which is the architecture's "who pays for proof" theme arriving at the apex without any new machinery.

It does not preserve the intended reader experience. The reader-experience node requires that "every doubt should remain actionable in a later Book." Under A the doubt is permanent, inspectable, and deliberately not actionable — the settlement holds and the witness cannot be re-tested. That is a direct contradiction of the node, not a variation on it. A also produces a witness who is unchallengeable, which is not the same as reliable but is adjacent to the comfort the brief withholds, and the collaborator does not address the difference. And "settled correctly" asks the reader to verify and conclude, whereas the architecture's aesthetic is that the narration never vouches and the reader learns to grade. Verifying is not the brief's affect; the cost of reliance disappears when the reader can check.

It makes Book 100's reform inevitable, and in the worst way: not inevitable because the logic runs out, but inevitable because the practice was already operating. The architecture at least leaves repeal of the exemption as a live alternative on paper. A narrows it, because a profession that has successfully operated under the exemption has less reason to remove it. The collaborator is alert to the adjacent risk in its check-me list — that the ending might read as "authority is whatever is obeyed" — but it does not connect that risk to its own recommendation.

One omission rather than an addition: the collaborator never touches Pass A's commitment-1 strain. Under the permissive reading of defect transfer, a witness can be admissible on a mentor's spent defect without ever having been shown wrong. Direction A takes that already-strained reading and makes it the foundation of a public settlement. That is the one place where the repair compounds an existing tension with an author commitment, and the brief does not notice.

## 6. Where Pass A and the collaborator disagree

Six substantive disagreements, and the support splits.

*What the symmetrical pair means.* Pass A: tragedy. Collaborator: non-choice. Pass A is better supported by the architecture's intent, which is institutional tragedy with a defensible fatal act; the collaborator is better supported by the architecture's execution, which supplies no agent. Same observation, opposite valuation, and neither is simply wrong.

*Where the missing decision belongs.* Pass A locates it at Book 24, where retroactivity should be argued and chosen rather than discovered; the collaborator locates it at 76, moved from doctrine to conduct. Pass A is better supported on entailment: 24 is the only node that stages an argument, and it is the only node with a real loser's cost in the document. The collaborator is better supported on the *need for an act*: Pass A's repair gives 76 a bill, and a bill is not a decision. The synthesis neither supplies: choose the scope at 24, so that the conduct fork at 76 is a choice made against a known constitution rather than a second decision after a settled one.

*Whether Book 24 is a strength or a setup.* Pass A calls the exemption the strongest thing in either document and its argument the strongest sentence. The collaborator files it under "setups." Pass A is better supported: a constitution is not a setup for the Book that tests it; it is the cause. The collaborator's vocabulary costs it precision, and the imprecision is what lets it treat scope as dismissible.

*What offers false reliability at 76.* Pass A: the exempt certifier, following the architecture's own node. Collaborator: the audit itself, "complete, honest, exhaustive." This is the collaborator's best original contribution. It is an addition, not a replacement — the exempt certifier remains the standing candidate from 24 — but "the audit as the false reliable object" is sharper for this Book than anything in the architecture.

*Whether the cost needs a new bearer or an existing one.* Both agree the cost has no bearer; this is the strongest convergence in the two documents and the best evidence that the hole is real, since they did not coordinate. The collaborator supplies a bearer; Pass A supplies the instrument (the spent mentor) and the requirement that the reader be able to name who paid. The collaborator's bearer is new and its permanence is invented; Pass A's instrument is already built and unwired. Pass A's is the better-supported repair, and the collaborator's instinct is the better-supported diagnosis.

*The horizon.* Pass A shows the architecture's proposed 60-Book cut is backwards, because it removes the excluded cohort, which is the only interval whose length is justified. The collaborator, under Direction B, says "the cut from 100 to 60 would become more attractive," repeating the architecture's error without testing it. Pass A is better supported.

Pass A is also the only one of the three documents to notice the commitment-1 strain, and the only one to separate finality from warrant, which is the distinction the whole late spine runs on. The collaborator is the only one to notice that an authority never exercised is not an authority, and the only one to put a person at the cost. Those are different parts of the problem, and they are not interchangeable.

## 7. Verdict

Pass A's diagnosis is better supported by the architecture, and the collaborator's is better supported by the scene, which is why neither can be adopted whole. Pass A correctly separates finality from warrant, identifies the exemption's unstated scope as the hinge, locates the only staged argument at Book 24, catches the commitment-1 strain, and shows that the ending quietly substitutes accountability for auditability. The collaborator correctly insists that a focal Book needs an act rather than a bill, that an authority never exercised is decorative, that the audit itself is the false reliable object at 76, and that the cost must land on a named person. But its repair rests on an act/ratify scope position the architecture does not contain, on an audit that is a verdict rather than a report, on a permanence its own ending cancels, and on a refusal it has defined as irresponsible — and it makes Book 100 more inevitable rather than less. The strongest single conclusion about dramatic decision construction here is that this architecture can generate consequences from a constitution but not choices within one: Book 24 argues without a loser, Book 50 and Book 100 decide without an actor, and Book 76 derives without an alternative, so five of the six focal nodes are settlements of one argument rather than contests inside it. The fix is therefore not a conduct fork bolted onto 76 and not a recognizer added to a discovery. It is to place the choice where the argument is — retroactivity argued and lost at 24 — and to give each existing argument a named person who wants the branch the profession does not take: at 24, someone who argues for certification and loses; at 76, someone who wants to destroy the archive or extend the exemption and is defeated by people who want the other. Neither document supplies both halves, and the evidence does not support declaring either one the winner.
